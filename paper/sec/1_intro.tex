\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Recovering a dense physical field from sparse, irregularly placed samples is a
recurring inverse problem in vision. Radio maps are an instance of it that is
unusually well posed for visual reasoning, because the field is shaped by geometry
that is directly observable. A transmitter illuminates a scene, walls absorb and
diffract the wavefront, and the received signal strength at a pixel is largely
determined by what lies along the straight path back to the source. The structure is
the same one that governs shading and shadowing, and it is visible in a floor plan.

Yet the methods that dominate this problem are evaluated in a regime that does not
occur. Learned reconstructors such as RadioUNet~\cite{levie2021radiounet} and
PMNet~\cite{lee2024pmnet} are trained and benchmarked on ray-traced simulations in
which samples are drawn uniformly at random and the transmitter location is supplied
as an input. Real measurements violate both assumptions. They are crowdsensed, which
means they arrive along the paths people actually walk and leave whole regions with no
observation at all, and the transmitter is frequently unlocatable because indoor
boosters share cell identifiers. Reported accuracy under the benchmark regime therefore
overstates what these models deliver once deployed.

We study the deployed regime directly, on real indoor cellular measurements alongside
the standard synthetic benchmark, and we find that the gap is not a matter of degree.
Three properties of real crowdsensed data are absent from the simulation setting and
each one changes what a model should condition on.

\noindent\textbf{Walls attenuate rather than block.} On real data, pairs of
measurements separated by a wall differ by an additional $2$ to $3$~dB at matched
distance. The wall does not terminate the signal, it casts a soft shadow whose depth
grows with the amount of material traversed. Convolutional backbones cannot construct
this quantity: it is a long, thin integral along a line joining two points, whereas a
receptive field aggregates over a neighbourhood. We show that unrelated architectures
plateau at the \emph{same} non-line-of-sight error, which is the signature of a missing
input rather than of insufficient capacity, and we supply the missing input as a
differentiable ray-occlusion field.

\noindent\textbf{Measurement order carries signal.} Crowdsensed samples are not a set.
They are a walk, and consecutive readings are correlated at $0.984$. A model that
consumes the same measurements as an ordered sequence reaches $1.79$~dB against
$9.79$~dB for an order-blind model on the same data ($p = 4.7\!\times\!10^{-23}$), and
the effect transfers across sites.

\noindent\textbf{The sensor is not calibrated.} Handsets disagree by a median of
$4.1$~dB and by as much as $27.8$~dB on the same signal. Treating measurements as
absolute, as every simulation-trained model does, injects an error larger than the
quantity being predicted.

These three observations motivate \methodname, a geometry-conditioned reconstructor
that takes the acquisition process as part of its input rather than as a nuisance. The
model conditions on a ray-occlusion field that integrates building occupancy along the
transmitter-to-pixel segment, on the order in which measurements were collected, and on
a per-device offset estimated jointly with the field. It predicts a dense map together
with a calibrated per-pixel uncertainty, which we then use to decide where to measure
next.

On the synthetic benchmark, \methodname matches the strongest learned reconstructor on
accuracy at $9.97$~dB free-unobserved RMSE against $10.00$~dB, attains the best
structural fidelity at $0.814$ SSIM, and is the only method in the comparison that also
supplies uncertainty which survives calibration. Nine deep and classical baselines are
evaluated under an identical protocol with bootstrap confidence intervals and paired
tests. We report an outcome that the benchmark framing would have concealed: on sparse
real measurements, classical interpolation remains the stronger reconstructor at
$5.51$~dB against $6.30$~dB. We therefore do not claim learned reconstruction as the
contribution. The contribution is the geometry, order and calibration structure that
learning does supply, each validated independently on real data, and the demonstration
that uncertainty derived from it guides acquisition better than space-filling
alternatives.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt,parsep=0pt]
    \item A ray-occlusion field that gives a convolutional reconstructor the
    line-integral geometry it cannot otherwise form, motivated by a diagnostic showing
    that unrelated backbones share one non-line-of-sight plateau.
    \item Order-aware and device-aware conditioning, each isolated on real indoor
    cellular data with negative controls, and a transmitter-agnostic design justified
    by the finding that only $18\%$ of cells yield a reliable point source.
    \item Calibrated per-pixel uncertainty, recalibrated to near-nominal coverage, and
    an acquisition rule built on it that outperforms space-filling collection on walled
    sites ($p = 0.039$).
    \item An evaluation on real crowdsensed measurements alongside the standard
    synthetic benchmark, which quantifies how far the two regimes diverge and states
    explicitly where learning does and does not help.
\end{enumerate}
